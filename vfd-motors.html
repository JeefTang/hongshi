VFD-duty motors · 0.55–315 kW · YVF2 / YXVF

A motor on a drive is not a standard motor

A variable-frequency drive changes more than the speed. It changes how the motor is cooled at low speed, what the winding insulation has to survive, where the current goes in the bearings, and which part of the torque curve you are allowed to use. The YVF2 / YXVF series is built for that duty from the frame up.

Four things a general-purpose motor gets wrong on a drive

Bolting a VFD onto a standard YE3 works — for a fan running at 80–100% speed. It stops working when the duty spends real hours at low speed, in a hot plant, on a long cable, or at constant torque. The failure is rarely immediate; it shows up as a winding that smells hot after eighteen months.

What the drive doesWhat it breaks on a standard motorWhat the VFD motor does instead
Runs below base speedThe shaft-mounted fan moves almost no air at 30% speed, so the winding cooks at exactly the moment the load is highestIndependent constant-speed cooling fan (IC416) — full airflow at any rotor speed
Switches at 4–16 kHzPWM pulses arrive as a steep wavefront; on a long cable the reflected wave can nearly double the peak voltage at the terminalsInverter-grade magnet wire and reinforced turn/phase insulation, rated for the pulse stress
Creates common-mode voltageShaft voltage discharges through the bearings and erodes the racewayInsulated bearing on the non-drive end and a shaft grounding path on the larger frames
Holds constant torque at low speedSelf-ventilation and the standard thermal margin run out; the motor cannot hold the loadThermal design and fan sized for continuous constant-torque duty across the stated range

This is why the two products are not interchangeable on paper even when the nameplate kW matches. The frame may be the same; the cooling, insulation system and bearing arrangement are not.

Torque, speed and the limits in between

A drive-duty motor is normally sold as constant torque below base speed and constant power above it. What that means for a pump is simple: below base speed you can hold any head the pump can make at that speed; above it you get more flow but the torque falls away.

A pump or fan load is quadratic: at 80% speed the shaft power is about half, at 50% speed about one eighth. That is the whole economic case for the drive — the motor only has to survive the trip down there.

When a standard motor plus a drive is enough

We would rather sell the cheaper answer when it is the right one. A general-purpose YE3 or YE4 on a drive is a sensible choice when all of the following hold:

  • The duty stays above roughly 70% of rated speed for most running hours
  • The load is quadratic (centrifugal pump or fan), not constant torque
  • Starts and stops are infrequent, and the ambient is normal
  • The motor-to-drive cable is short and the drive output is filtered if it is not

Specify VFD duty when any of these is true:

  • Continuous operation below about 50% speed
  • Constant-torque load — positive-displacement pumps, conveyors, mixers, crushers
  • High ambient, high altitude, or a hot enclosure
  • Frequent starts, high switching frequency, or a long cable run to the motor

What we need to quote one

  • Duty point — kW at the shaft at rated speed, and the driven machine's curve
  • Speed range — minimum and maximum operating speed, and how long is spent at each
  • Load type — quadratic or constant torque; starting torque if it is heavy
  • Supply — voltage, frequency, phases; 380 V or 6/10 kV
  • Drive — make and model, or let us select it with the motor
  • Installation — cable length from drive to motor, ambient, altitude, indoor/outdoor, IP requirement
  • Area classification — safe area, or the Ex zone and gas group
  • Extras — encoder, winding and bearing RTDs, space heaters, brake, terminal box position

Send the existing nameplate and the drive model and we will come back with the equivalent YVF2 / YXVF frame, the cooling arrangement, the minimum continuous speed and the torque available at your lowest operating point.

As a package

Motor, drive, panel and pump as one train

The failure modes on a VFD-driven train are interface failures: the drive's overload setting against the motor's thermal time constant, the cable against the winding insulation, the minimum speed against the pump's bearing film. Buying them from one place puts that interface on one drawing. The motor can also ship on its own, with or without a drive — a replacement on a palm oil mill's EFB line or screw press is the usual case.

01 · Drive selection

Sized to the duty, not the nameplate

Drive, breaker, line reactor, cooling and terminals assembled and tested as a panel, not shipped as loose components.

04 · Commissioning

Aligned and run before shipment

Pump, motor, coupling and baseplate aligned; the train run at minimum, base and maximum speed before it leaves.

Compliance

Efficiency, and what we will not claim

The IE classes in GB 18613-2020 are written for line-start machines. For a motor running from a drive, the meaningful figure is the type-test efficiency at the speed and load where it actually runs, and we state the standard and the test point it was measured against. We do not label a VFD-duty motor with an IE class borrowed from its line-start equivalent. Market certifications — CE, EAC, UL/CSA or a hazardous-area approval — are quoted item by item against the motor, the drive, the voltage and the country of installation.

Tell us the speed range, not just the kW.

Minimum speed, maximum speed, load type and cable length decide whether the answer is a standard motor on a drive or a VFD-duty machine. We will tell you which, and why.
